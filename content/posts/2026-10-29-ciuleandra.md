Romanian literature isn’t particularly well known outside Romania. I suspect Bulgarian, Serbian, and Albanian readers could say something similar about their own. The same probably goes for most smaller language communities. That feels like a shame, as there are books here that deserve a much wider readership. Well, unless you're an author from a Nordic country writing Nordic noir thrillers. Then your works get translated more easily.

> There's a book loosely related to this topic, [*Translating the Literatures of Small European Nations*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvvb7kq9), with some interesting insights. But this article is not about that.

Now, back to Romanian literature. There is *this guy*, [Liviu Rebreanu](https://ro.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liviu_Rebreanu), who wrote two of my favorite novels: *Ciuleandra* and [*Pădurea spânzuraților*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_of_the_Hanged_(novel)). *Pădurea spânzuraților* roughly translates to *Forest of the Hanged*, while *Ciuleandra*, the thing we're going to talk about, is best left untranslated and deserves a separate explanation.

Ciuleandra is a traditional dance from southern Romania, with a rhythm that gradually accelerates. It begins slowly and deliberately, like a cat stalking its prey. Then the music quickens, the dancers follow, and everything builds toward a frenzy.

It’s very Balkan/southern Romanian, and easier to show than to explain:

For me, that accelerating rhythm is also the key to the novel.

The story opens with Puiu Faranga, a young Romanian aristocrat, killing his *blameless* and extremely beautiful wife in an apparently senseless act. His father, desperate to save him from prison, arranges for him to enter a sanatorium and pretend to be insane. There, Puiu comes under the care of a doctor who has to work out what lies behind the crime. Is it pure madness, obsession, or jealousy?

As Puiu revisits his marriage and the events that led to the murder, the dance becomes an obsession.

The distinction between the madness he is supposed to perform and what he actually experiences grows increasingly difficult to draw. What interests me most is this unraveling: his father can arrange an escape from the consequences, but Puiu still has to live with what he has done.

For readers of English, there is Gabi Reigh’s translation, published by Cadmus Press in 2021.

Alternatively, there is Sergiu Nicolaescu’s 1985 film adaptation, which I found largely faithful to the book, with a few differences. You can give it a try:

Don’t expect a blockbuster or a slick psychological thriller starring Michael Douglas. The film takes its time. Scenes linger, and the performances have a theatrical quality that may take some getting used to. You have to let each scene settle before moving on to the next. If you’re unfamiliar with this style of filmmaking, it may test your patience. But if you stay with it, the ending makes the slow build worthwhile.

By the end, you will have more questions than answers:

- Is the wife really blameless and pure? How much do we actually know about her beyond Puiu's recollections? Does his obsession reveal her, or keep replacing her with his own ideal version?

- Puiu's memories are an unreliable layer of defense. When he revisits his marriage, is he remembering, explaining, or trying to make himself bearable... to himself?

- Is the father's protection another form of control? Is everything related to control?

- Was he mad when he killed her, or did he become mad by the end of the novel/movie? Did the doctor cure him of the lies but infect him with the truth?

We can only speculate.
