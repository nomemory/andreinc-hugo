> As a child, I was never a fan of any actor or singer. David Bowie is the closest thing to a "music idol" I've ever had. I never had a poster of him on my wall, or a T-shirt with his face on it, but he was a constant presence in my playlists, to the point where Spotify calls him my favourite artist.

11 years ago, on 19 November 2015, David Bowie releases ["Blackstar"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackstar_(song)) (as a single). Early the following year, on 8 January, [the album of the same name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackstar_(album)) is released. Oh, what a great album, if not the greatest of them all. It's certainly one of my top five favourite (concept) albums, maybe even top three.

Two days after the album's release, on 10 January, in an act of biological defiance, surprising us all, Bowie suddenly dies. It was a sad surprise for anyone outside his closest circles. He kept the secret of that <button type="button" class="image-popup-trigger" popovertarget="ormen-cell" aria-haspopup="dialog">nasty illness</button> well.

When I heard the news, I felt weird for at least 10 minutes and, in an ego-driven cascade of thoughts, realised I would never be able to "experience" his music at a live concert, and that the great album he had just released would be his last. It was a new feeling. I was still young enough (in my late 20s) that most of the "characters" from my childhood and teenage years were still alive. He was the first of the gang to die, and his name wasn't Hector, but David. My first impulse after hearing the news was to listen to "Thursday's Child" on repeat. So I did.

Did he read the reviews before his time had come?

---

When Bowie was writing the song, he was seriously ill. That song and that video are filled with mortality, and therefore with mystery, because death itself is even more mysterious than life: an unnecessary act of unmaking something (life) that is already mysterious. `</philosophy>`

*Blackstar* starts with the cryptic verses: 

> In the villa of <button class="ormen-name-toggle" type="button" data-ormen-toggle aria-pressed="false" aria-label="Change Ormen to Allmen">Ormen</button>, in the villa of <button class="ormen-name-toggle" type="button" data-ormen-toggle aria-pressed="false" aria-label="Change Ormen to Allmen">Ormen</button>

> Stands a solitary candle, ah-ah, ah-ah

> In the centre of it all, in the centre of it all

> **Your eyes**

... the lyrics are <button type="button" class="image-popup-trigger" popovertarget="ormen-yeats-poem" aria-haspopup="dialog"><em>Yeats</em>-like</button>, or maybe <button type="button" class="image-popup-trigger" popovertarget="ormen-eliot-poem" aria-haspopup="dialog"><em>Eliot</em>-like</button>:

<button type="button" class="image-popup-trigger" popovertarget="ormen-yeats-poem" aria-haspopup="dialog"><em>Yeats</em>-like</button> because, just as Bowie does here, *Yeats* manages to transform a simple image into something of cosmic significance:

The language itself seems to circle an invisible object: *your eyes*.

And for a moment I wondered whether ***Your eyes*** might be the eyes of Hermione Farthingale, <button type="button" class="image-popup-trigger" popovertarget="ormen-mousy-hair" aria-haspopup="dialog">the girl with the mousy hair</button>, to whom he dedicated <button type="button" class="image-popup-trigger" popovertarget="ormen-letter" aria-haspopup="dialog">a letter, or a song, a sad song</button> about a love that happened and didn't quite happen in the end, unrequited or unassumed. What if *her eyes*, which are not *his eyes*, were the eyes he was seeing in the *Villa of Ormen*?

Could he be hinting at her in another song, all those years later? Maybe the artist, as death approaches, sees the imaginary eyes of the muse of his youth, at the center of it all.

Or maybe there is closure, and it's not about a faded muse of youthful years. 

Those *your eyes* he is speaking about are *his own eyes* staring at him from within. After all, in the video we see a Bowie who is blind and fragile. 

His eyesight, now external, is at the centre of it all.

Before the final cut, *The Villa of Ormen* was in fact *The Villa of Allmen*, so maybe this song is about all of us, and more than one Hermione meets the eye.

<div class="ormen-eyes ormen-manuscript">

</div>

The song should be about "all men" and that villa of the serpent. 

"Ormen" means "serpent" in Norwegian. The serpent is temptation and death in most cultures. 

So the Villa of Ormen might be a symbolic world where mortality is universal and existence is continually transformed (have you seen the wax flowing and dripping in the video? It's like clay). 

> Did you know that when Hermione left Bowie in '69, she went to work on a film called [Song for Norway](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0066393/)? "Ormen" means "serpent" in Norwegian.

The solitary candle is a tiny source of light, surrounded by darkness (unnamed, undescribed, "at the centre of it all"), an image that works remarkably well as a metaphor for "human consciousness".

> Did you know that in 2013, during his tour, David Bowie wore a T-shirt with **Song of Norway** written on it? The same T-shirt appears in the video for "Where Are We Now?"

So a muse cannot be forgotten, and that's an interesting choice of a T-shirt.

> Something happened on the day he died

> Spirit rose a metre and stepped aside

> Somebody else took his place, and bravely cried

> ""I'm a blackstar, I'm a blackstar.""

And in the video you can see poor *Major Tom*, the astronaut introduced in Bowie's 1969 song Space Oddity. This is particularly sad because Major Tom was one of Bowie's earliest creations. Almost half a century later, we see him dead. In death, wandering through the Universe, he has landed on an alien planet, alienated near a "blackstar" that looks like an eclipse.

The jeweled skull, his skull, becomes a sacred relic. My reading is that the man has died, but his artistic identity has become immortal. The women carrying the skull are preserving something that survives its creator, that survives *Major Tom*.

Eventually, the skull is then taken to a mysterious settlement where women with crazy dancing moves participate in a ritual. 

Bowie suddenly becomes a priest, or an inquisitor.

<div class="ormen-eyes ormen-priest">

This resembles an ancient religious ceremony, but its purpose is deliberately ambiguous. Or not. 

A new *Blackstar* is being born, after the previous black star has died.

--- 

> OK, ok, this article is not about Blackstar in general. This is about the Villa of Ormen. 

The Villa of Ormen is not a place to be found.

The Villa of Ormen is the final chamber of human existence, where identity dissolves, the body perishes, and something indefinable remains. 

Bowie, standing at the edge of his own mortality, invites us into this strange, sacred space. 

We search its shadows for the meaning of his death, for some final revelation from the artist who spent a lifetime reinventing himself. Yet his last great trick is to turn the mystery upon us. For at the centre of it all, illuminated by that solitary candle, **the eyes are not Bowie's.**
